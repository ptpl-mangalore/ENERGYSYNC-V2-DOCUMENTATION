Topics

Problem: Biased ERP Market Research
The team's research is biased, relying on flawed summaries from AI/Google instead of analyzing granular features.
This creates a "mini-me" LLM effect, filtering out valuable data and missing opportunities.

Example: The assumption that Oracle is "finance-centric" is disproven by its module list, which includes Procurement, Supply Chain, and Inventory.
Risk: This approach causes "feature cherry-picking," leading to a product that is a mere copy of competitors instead of a unique solution leveraging the team's "tribal knowledge" in utilities.
Solution: A Two-Phase Research Methodology

Phase 1: Broad Collection
Collect all features and modules from a wide range of competitors (e.g., SAP, Oracle, MS Dynamics, IFS, Acumatica).
The goal is a comprehensive baseline, not immediate filtering.
Deliverable: A comparison matrix showing all features, including those that are missing from a competitor (marked with an "X"), as these gaps are as informative as inclusions.
Phase 2: Deep-Dive & Filtering
After the baseline is complete, deep-dive into specific features (e.g., "Purchase Requisition").
Analyze the specific implementation and dependencies of that feature across competitors.

Criteria:
Client-specific vs. generic
Vendor-specific vs. generic
User-specific vs. generic
Scalability and field limits
Modular dependencies
Outcome: A filtered list of "must-have" features, which forms the core product definition.
Goal: Modular ERP Architecture
The product's goal is not to rebuild a monolith but to create a modular, indigenous system.
This requires analyzing competitors' modularity and compatibility to build a system that can plug into existing client infrastructure.

Key Questions:
How do competitor modules behave independently?
How are they interconnected?
Are there mandatory core modules, with others as plug-and-play?
Can our modules be compatible with existing client systems (e.g., a 20-year-old SAP system)?
